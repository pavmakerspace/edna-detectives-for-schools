eDNA Detectives

Ages 12–1460–75 minutesGroups of 2–3

Driving question

How Do You Find a Creature Without Ever Seeing It?

Learning intention

Detect hidden life in water from the traces it leaves, and use evidence to identify what cannot be seen.

Learning outcomes

  • Do. Combine a trace with a habitat clue to identify an organism, and judge how reliable that call is.
  • Think. Explain how eDNA provides evidence of organisms, and how it is used to study biodiversity.
  • Feel. Value responsible science and protecting water, local and global.

Curriculum links: Science & Ocean Literacy

Ocean Literacy. The ocean holds a great diversity of life, and much of it is still unexplored.

Science inquiry skills. Observe, infer from evidence, weigh its reliability and limits, explain and communicate.

Sustainability: UN SDG 14, Life Below Water. Protecting marine biodiversity through responsible, non-invasive science.

What you need

Print one Learner Activity per student or pair and provide pens. A shared screen and internet access for the linked video clips; the eDNA Detectives game is optional.

Teaching without a screen: Read the worksheet introduction aloud, then use the Investigate activity. All four tasks can be completed without the videos or game.

Facilitation approach

Invite students to notice, discuss and make a claim before supplying an explanation. Ask them to point to the evidence behind their ideas, consider alternative explanations and say what would help them feel more confident. Welcome different answers when students can support their reasoning.

Before you begin

Connect to a familiar place: Start with a local river, pond or coast. Explain that living things leave DNA in their surroundings through skin, mucus, waste and other material. Scientists collect and study these traces.

Model evidence-based reasoning: In the Investigate activity, ask students which clue supports their choice and which candidates it rules out. Explain that the puzzle models combining clues; real eDNA identification involves analysing DNA and comparing it with reference sequences.

Discuss what a result means: In the Reflect activity, ask “What does this evidence support, and what else could explain it?” DNA can move with water, break down or come from food; libraries of known DNA can also have gaps. A match alone does not establish that a live animal is at the sampling site now.

Facilitation Guide

Explore, Investigate, Reflect, Create

1. Explore15 min

Learner Activity

Ask: “How could we find a hidden animal without seeing it?” Give one minute to think, then share. Play On Mission: eDNA or a relevant excerpt and use its worksheet question; pause for pairs to share an observation. Or read the worksheet introduction. Pairs complete the Explore activity; check together.

Key Questions & Prompts

SEE: What did you notice in the clip or reading?
THINK: How could a DNA trace help investigate hidden life?
WONDER: What would you need to check before drawing a conclusion?

Evidence of Learning

Sequence how eDNA is collected and read; explain why depth and place matter.

2. Investigate15 min

Learner Activity

Pairs complete the Investigate activity warm-up and explain their choice. If time and devices allow, play the game instead; discuss how the puzzle models clues, while real identification analyses DNA.

Key Questions & Prompts

• What traces reveal a creature?
• Why is one trace not enough?
• How does a habitat clue rule one out?

Evidence of Learning

Combine trace and habitat evidence to eliminate candidates by weighing more than one clue.

3. Reflect15 min

Learner Activity

Play What is eDNA or a relevant excerpt and discuss its worksheet question. Groups tackle the Reflect activity, compare explanations and confidence, and suggest a follow-up check. Invite two groups to defend different explanations that fit the evidence.

Key Questions & Prompts

• What did the evidence rule out?
• When is one trace not enough?
• What if the species isn't in the library yet?

Evidence of Learning

Distinguish a valid DNA match from claims about a live animal’s present location, exact count or source.

4. Create15 min

Learner Activity

Play Citizens of the Sea or a relevant excerpt and discuss its worksheet question. Groups of 2–3 complete the Create activity, choosing sampling locations and a follow-up. Each gives a one-minute pitch; peers name one strength or question.

Key Questions & Prompts

• Which habitat, and what might a sample show?
• Why be careful about the limit you picked?
• How could this protect the habitat?

Evidence of Learning

Apply eDNA to a real habitat, state a limit, justify a conservation benefit.

See, Think, Wonder

Opens Explore: move from noticing to inquiry. Give quiet time to notice before inviting explanations. (Harvard Project Zero routine.)

Weigh More Than One Clue

A single trace fits many creatures. Pair it with a habitat clue to narrow the field, and treat a match as a strong clue to weigh. The core habit of the lesson.

After the session

Notice: where curiosity sparked, how learners used evidence to rule out, whether they linked eDNA to local water, and how clearly they named its limits.

Answer key: Learner Activity

Explore activity: sheds DNA → collect a sample at a depth and place → separate the DNA → read and match to a library.

Investigate activity: Clue 1 (deep, no sunlight) rules out yellowfin tuna and green sea turtle. Clue 2 (glowing cloud to distract a predator) rules out the anglerfish (a lure to hunt, not a cloud). Answer: vampire squid.

Reflect activity: Matching DNA detected: S. A live fish is there now: N. Food is a possible source: P. Exactly three fish: N. A valid match alone cannot establish source, live location or count. Compare upstream and downstream samples, repeat sampling, use contamination controls and seek independent observations. Reasoned alternatives are welcome.

Create activity: Look for a justified sampling location, a limitation, a follow-up and a realistic way to help protect the habitat. Example: sample several points upstream and downstream of a mangrove, repeat with controls, and use a detection to target further surveys; a non-detection does not prove absence.

Timing: Four 15-minute stages form the 60-minute core. For 75 minutes, add 10 minutes for pitches and 5 for an exit ticket: “What does a DNA match support, and what remains uncertain?” For older students, discuss false positives, controls and non-detection.

Preparation and access: Print one worksheet per learner or pair; provide pens. Preview the clips and game and check captions. Play each clip, or a relevant excerpt, before its linked question: On Mission for Explore, What is eDNA for Reflect, and Citizens of the Sea for Create. Check running times in advance and select excerpts to fit the 60-minute plan; allow extra time for full clips and discussion. No screen: read the worksheet introduction and use the Investigate activity. Students may discuss or dictate answers. Sources for background: NOAA: environmental DNA; MBARI: eDNA.
